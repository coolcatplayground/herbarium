This is a plain text file. Open and edit it with Notepad, TextEdit, or
any text editor — no coding required.

HOW THIS WORKS
  - Each Pokémon gets one block: starts with "### <name>" and ends with
    "### END".
  - Use the Pokémon's name exactly as PokéAPI/the Pokédex spells it, all
    lowercase, hyphens instead of spaces or apostrophes.
      e.g. bulbasaur, mega-venusaur, exeggutor-alola, mr-mime
  - Fill in the four fields inside the block:
      binomial          — your invented "scientific" name for the specimen
      plant_analogue    — the real plant (or plant group) it echoes
      genetic_concept   — a short tag naming the biology/genetics concept
      note              — the explanation. Can run across several lines —
                           just keep typing, it all gets joined together
                           when the app reads it. Leave it unindented.
      record            — optional. What the in-game Pokédex has reported
                           about this species across every generation, put
                           together in your own words and weighed against real
                           research. Shown on the specimen page as "Collected
                           Observations", above the habitat. The app lists the
                           games it came from automatically, so you don't need
                           to — but naming a specific one inline is fine where
                           it matters. Species-level, like "note": a Mega or
                           regional form inherits it.
      habitat_note      — optional. What THIS specimen contributes to the
                           habitat it's filed under, as opposed to what the
                           habitat is (that lives in habitatMap.js). Shown
                           on the specimen page under the habitat panel.
                           Unlike "note", this one is never inherited by a
                           Mega or regional form, because those often sit in
                           a different habitat than their base species — give
                           such a form its own block with just this field if
                           it needs one.
  - To add a brand-new Pokémon, copy the TEMPLATE block below, paste it
    at the bottom of the file, and fill it in. Order doesn't matter.
  - Don't delete the "###" or "### END" lines — the app uses those to
    find where each entry starts and stops. Everything else is yours to
    edit freely.
  - Save the file, then refresh the app in your browser to see changes.
    If the app is deployed on GitHub Pages, you'll need to commit and
    push this file for the live site to update.

### bulbasaur
binomial: Bulbasaur cormus-dorsalis
plant_analogue: Corms & bulbs (e.g. Crocus, Gladiolus)
genetic_concept: Vegetative storage organs
note: The bulb it carries is drawn as a corm, and a corm is not a bulb. Cut an onion and the rings are leaf bases packed around a tiny stem; cut a crocus corm and there is nothing but solid stem tissue, no layers at all. The corm is also annual — the plant spends the whole of it in a season and builds a replacement directly on top of the exhausted one, so the store climbs a little further out of the ground each year. Contractile roots haul it back down. The organ that feeds the plant would surface and die without a second organ whose only job is to keep pulling it under.
habitat_note: Stored food that cannot run away is the most heavily defended tissue a plant owns, and bulbs are the clearest case. A bulb is a concentrated, immobile, year-round larder sitting in soil full of things that would eat it, so the chemistry is correspondingly serious: daffodil bulbs carry lycorine and are poisonous enough that mistaking them for onions puts people in hospital most years, and autumn crocus corms contain colchicine, which has no antidote worth the name. The pattern is general. Where a plant has concentrated resources into one organ and cannot move it, defence follows the value — which is why the toxic parts of a plant are so often the parts we most want to eat, and why so much of early agriculture consisted of finding, breeding or processing our way around exactly this problem.
record: The entries are consistent from the beginning: a seed planted on its back at birth that grows along with it (Pokémon Red & Blue, 1998), filled with nutrients and swelling as the body does (Pokémon Gold & Silver, 2000), letting it go for days without eating (Pokémon Yellow, 1999). What that describes is a plant's actual relationship with its own storage, which is less like a larder and more like a bank account with a strict term. Perennials cycle carbon into storage organs at the end of a season and draw it down at the start of the next, and the drawdown is the risky part — a plant in spring is spending reserves on leaves that have not yet earned anything, running at a loss until the canopy is out and paying. Defoliate it at exactly that moment, before the account is back in credit, and it can be killed by a single well-timed grazing where three later in the year would do nothing. Carrying your own supply is not the same as being safe.
### END

### ivysaur
binomial: Ivysaur cormus-intermedius
plant_analogue: Pre-anthesis flower buds
genetic_concept: Photoperiodism
note: A bud that waits for the right moment is measuring something, and it is not day length, despite the name. Short-day plants are long-night plants: interrupt the darkness with a few minutes of light in the middle and they will not flower at all — which is how chrysanthemum growers hold a crop back for the market, and how a street lamp keeps one tree in leaf after its neighbours have dropped. The measuring is done by the leaves. The bud only receives the verdict, carried up to it as a protein through the phloem, and opens on instructions it had no part in writing.
habitat_note: The bud is the most valuable thing on this specimen and it is defended accordingly, which is a rule rather than an accident. Optimal defence theory predicts that a plant should protect tissue in proportion to what it is worth and how likely it is to be attacked, and the measurements bear it out with unusual consistency: young expanding leaves, buds, and reproductive parts carry the highest concentrations of defensive chemistry in the plant, and old leaves about to be shed carry the least. It makes plain economic sense. A mature leaf has already repaid its construction and has little future left to protect; a bud is entirely future, has repaid nothing, and losing it costs a whole season. So a plant will let its oldest foliage be eaten and spend heavily to keep something eating the tip.
record: The entries track a single process across generations: the bud grows by drawing energy and gives off an aroma when it is ready to bloom (Pokémon Yellow, 1999), with sunlight making the bud larger (Pokémon Gold & Silver, 2000) and a sweetening smell as evidence the flower will soon open. Scent arriving before the flower is real and it is a scheduling problem. Flowers are expensive and short-lived, so a plant gains nothing by opening before its pollinators are active or before neighbouring plants of its own species are ready — and floral volatiles begin to be emitted from buds shortly before anthesis, rising as the flower opens and often peaking on a daily rhythm timed to when its couriers fly. The advertising campaign starts before the product is available. What the entries describe as a bud beginning to smell sweet is a plant announcing a date.
### END

### venusaur
binomial: Venusaur magniflora
plant_analogue: Giant Rafflesia / Titan arum
genetic_concept: Heterochrony (shifted developmental timing)
note: Disproportion on this scale is a problem of timing before it is one of size. A titan arum spends years as a single leaf, banking starch into a corm that can pass seventy kilograms, then spends the lot at once on a flowering structure taller than a person — not one flower but thousands of small ones on a spike, heating itself to roughly blood temperature to drive the smell out into the night air. The bloom is not an addition to the plant. It is the corm, converted, which is why the plant is a much smaller thing the year after it flowers.
habitat_note: The largest flowers on Earth belong to a plant that has given up nearly everything else. Rafflesia has no leaves, no stem, no roots and no chlorophyll: it lives as a network of filaments threaded through the tissue of a Tetrastigma vine, entirely internal, invisible for most of its life, and it emerges only to flower — a single bloom up to a metre across and ten kilograms in weight, which smells of rotting meat, lasts a few days and is pollinated by carrion flies. It is arguably the most reduced flowering plant there is, having discarded every organ except the reproductive one. It has also been caught stealing: a substantial number of its genes turn out to have been acquired horizontally from its host, which is a thing plants are not supposed to be able to do. Enormous flowers are almost always paid for by something else being abandoned.
record: Every entry for three decades says the same thing in different words: the flower absorbs solar energy and converts it to power, and the Pokémon is stronger in summer and stays on the move to find sunlight (Pokémon Red & Blue, 1998). Moving toward light is something plants do, on a timescale that hides it. Phototropism is growth rather than motion — cells on the shaded side elongate more than those on the lit side, so the stem bends — and the mechanism was worked out by Darwin and his son, who established that the tip senses the light while the bending happens below it, implying a signal travelling down. That signal turned out to be auxin, redistributed toward the dark side. It is the first plant hormone anyone identified, and the experiment that found it was capping the tips of grass seedlings with tiny foil hats. A plant that stays on the move to seek sunlight is doing something real, several centimetres at a time.
### END

### oddish
binomial: Oddish nocturnus-radix
plant_analogue: Geophytes (underground bulbs/tubers)
genetic_concept: Circadian gene expression
note: Anything keeping to a schedule this exact is running a clock, and plants are where the clock was first caught. In 1729 the astronomer de Mairan shut a mimosa in a dark cupboard and found its leaves still opening in the morning and folding at night, with no sun to prompt them — the first evidence in any organism that the rhythm is internal. What runs it is a loop of genes switching each other off over roughly twenty-four hours, and in constant darkness it drifts slightly fast or slightly slow. The drift is the proof. A reaction to sunrise would keep perfect time; only a clock can be wrong.
habitat_note: A specimen whose whole life runs on a clock belongs to the least obvious part of chemical defence: the timing of it. Plant toxins are not held at a constant level. Nicotine production in tobacco rises at night, and in a range of species the jasmonate signalling that governs defence is under circadian control, so the plant is most heavily armed at the hours its herbivores actually feed and lets the concentration fall when they are not around. The reason is cost — defence is expensive and there is no point paying for it around the clock — and the effect is measurable in a way that has practical consequences. Caterpillars fed on plants whose internal clock is running in phase with their own do measurably worse than caterpillars given plants whose clock has been shifted out of step. Being poisonous at the right time of day is worth more than being poisonous all of it.
record: The entries are unusually specific about behaviour: by day it keeps its face buried in the ground, and at night it roams to scatter its seeds (Pokémon Red & Blue, 1998), awakened by moonlight (Pokémon Gold & Silver, 2000). Burying the photosynthetic part and travelling in the dark is exactly backwards for a plant, but the seed-scattering half is a real and under-appreciated strategy. Geocarpy — burying your own seed rather than dispersing it — is what a peanut does: after pollination the flower stalk elongates downward, drives the developing pod into the soil, and the crop matures underground. Several desert and Mediterranean species do the same, and the logic is that in an unreliable place the ground beneath the parent is the site you have most information about. Not every plant is trying to send its offspring somewhere else. Some are deliberately planting them where they already are.
### END

### gloom
binomial: Gloom nocturnus-nectaris
plant_analogue: Carrion flowers (Amorphophallus, Stapelia)
genetic_concept: Convergent floral chemistry
note: Convergence here is measured in a currency the flies pay. Several of these flowers are not merely attractive to carrion insects but convincing to them: blowflies land, feed, and lay their eggs on the bloom, and the larvae hatch onto a plant and starve. Fooling an insect's nose is one thing; fooling its decision about where to leave its offspring is a considerably harder test, and these pass it. The lineages that do are unrelated — arums, milkweeds, birthworts — arriving at the same rotting-meat chemistry from entirely different starting material, because the audience never changed.
habitat_note: The stink is a costume, and the wardrobe is remarkably specific. Carrion flowers — the titan arum, the starfish flowers of Stapelia, dead-horse arum, and a scattering of others across unrelated families — do not merely smell unpleasant, they emit the actual chemistry of decomposition: dimethyl disulfide and trisulfide, and in some species putrescine and cadaverine, which are the compounds a decaying body produces and which flies are built to find. Several go further and complete the illusion, warming the flower to body temperature, growing hair on it, and colouring it the mottled red-brown of meat. A few trap their visitors overnight and release them dusted with pollen in the morning. Independent lineages arrived at the same forgery because the audience is the same, and the flies are not paid anything at all.
record: The entries are unexpectedly precise about the fluid: what looks like drool is a sweet nectar used to attract prey (Pokémon Red & Blue, 1998), sticky and clinging, and the smell is foul enough that around one person in a thousand reportedly enjoys it (Pokémon Yellow, 1999). Sweet and repulsive at once is not a contradiction in real flowers; it is a signature. Plenty of strongly scented blooms carry sugary secretions alongside sulphurous volatiles, because the two are addressed to different senses and doing different jobs — the smell brings the fly in from a distance, and the sugar keeps it there long enough to be loaded. As for the one in a thousand, that is close to how these things actually distribute. Sensitivity to specific volatiles varies genetically across people to a startling degree — the classic case being the androstenone that some find foul, some find sweet, and some cannot smell at all — so a plant that smells unbearable to almost everyone will smell interesting to a few.
### END

### vileplume
binomial: Vileplume sporocarpus-maximus
plant_analogue: Puffball fungi & Lycopodium spores
genetic_concept: Spore dispersal & allergenicity
note: The largest petals in the world are the wrong shape for making anyone ill, and the distinction is a real one. Allergy comes overwhelmingly from plants that do not advertise — grasses, birch, ragweed — wind-pollinated species releasing pollen in enormous quantity, small and dry enough to stay airborne for days, because wind is a courier that cannot be aimed. A flower with an audience makes heavy sticky grains, comparatively few of them, and hands them over in person. Goldenrod is blamed every autumn for what ragweed does, on no evidence beyond being the thing in flower that people can see.
habitat_note: Its note reaches for club moss spores, and they are worth the detour because they are the most useful powder in botany. Lycopodium spores are extremely uniform in size, around thirty microns across and near-identical from plant to plant, and they are so water-repellent that a hand dipped into them comes out dry. Those two properties made them industrial. They were the original flash powder — dispersed in air and ignited, a cloud of them burns almost instantaneously, which is how early photography and stage lightning were done — and they served as a fingerprint dust, a pill coating, and a standard reference particle for calibrating instruments precisely because their dimensions could be relied upon. A plant that throws clouds of fine, dry, intensely hydrophobic powder is describing something real, and the reason it burns is the same reason flour and custard powder do.
record: The entries scale one variable and keep scaling it: the larger its petals, the more toxic pollen it holds, and the head is heavy and hard to hold up (Pokémon Red & Blue, 1998), with the world's largest petals shaking out heavy clouds of it at every step (Pokémon Gold & Silver, 2000). Big flowers really are structurally awkward, and plants that build them solve it by not holding them up. The largest blooms in the world sit at ground level — Rafflesia on the forest floor, the titan arum on a single enormous stalk that is not a stem, Aristolochia hanging from a vine — because a large flower held aloft on a slender support is a lever, and the material to make it rigid would cost more than the flower does. There is also a real trade-off behind the entry's arithmetic: pollen and petal compete for the same budget, and across species the ones that invest in enormous display generally produce relatively less pollen, not more, because the display is what the pollen is buying.
### END

### bellossom
binomial: Bellossom heliotropica
plant_analogue: Sun-tracking flowers (Helianthus)
genetic_concept: Heliotropism
note: Sunflowers are the usual example and they are also the usual mistake: a mature head does not follow the sun at all. The tracking happens while the stem is still growing, the shaded side elongating faster through the day and the lit side catching up overnight, and it stops when growth stops — leaving the head fixed, pointing east. That is not the mechanism failing. An east-facing head warms faster in the morning than one aimed anywhere else, and bees come to a warm flower first, so the plant that has stopped moving is still being paid for where it stopped.
habitat_note: Flowers that follow the sun are usually selling warmth. Arctic and alpine species — the mountain avens and Arctic poppies especially — hold their blooms as shallow parabolic dishes and track through the day, concentrating sunlight onto the reproductive parts at the centre. The inside of the dish can run several degrees above the surrounding air, which speeds the flower's own development and, just as usefully, makes it somewhere insects come to sit and warm up. In a cold place the nectar is not the whole offer. The flower is also selling a heated seat, and collecting pollination as the fee.
record: The entries are unusually observant about colour: the flower petals deepen through exposure to sunlight (Pokémon Emerald, 2005), and the leaves start spinning when it gets plenty of it (Pokémon Ruby & Sapphire, 2003). Light really does set pigment. The anthocyanins responsible for red, purple and blue in flowers and fruit are made only when the tissue is lit — the enzymes are switched on downstream of light-sensing photoreceptors — which is why the shaded side of an apple stays green while the exposed cheek reddens, and why fruit growers thin leaves and lay reflective sheeting on orchard floors specifically to colour up the crop. It also explains the folklore in the earliest entry, that the dance is a ritual to summon the sun (Pokémon Gold & Silver, 2000). The relationship runs the other way, but the correlation is real enough that people would notice it.
### END

### tangela
binomial: Tangela vine-confertus
plant_analogue: Bindweed & kudzu (Convolvulaceae)
genetic_concept: Indeterminate vine growth
note: A tangle is a search pattern that has run out of things to find. A climbing shoot does not grow straight and then bend when it meets something — the tip sweeps a slow circle as it extends, hours to a revolution, feeling for contact, which Darwin established by tracing the movement on a glass plate. Touch is what stops it. A tendril that brushes a support coils around it within minutes, then twists the free length into a helix with a reversal halfway along, because a coil without a reversal cannot shorten without spinning the whole plant. The spring is the point: it hauls the shoot in, and gives when the wind takes the support.
habitat_note: A vine is the mesophyte strategy pushed to its logical end: don't build a trunk, borrow one. A self-supporting plant spends most of its carbon on the wood that holds its leaves up, and a climber spends almost none — which buys it length and foliage instead, and is why a liana reaches the canopy years faster than the tree it is using and why kudzu can put on the better part of a foot in a day. The theft is structural rather than nutritional; nothing is taken out of the host's sap. It is simply that holding yourself up is the single most expensive thing a plant does, and this specimen has declined to pay for it.
record: The entries have circled one property since 1998: the vines never stop growing (Pokémon Yellow, 1999), they snap off painlessly if grabbed and are replaced by new growth the very next day (Pokémon Ruby & Sapphire, 2003). Both halves of that are ordinary plant behaviour, and the reason is a thing animals gave up. A plant keeps embryonic tissue for life. Meristems at every shoot tip and root tip stay permanently undifferentiated, dividing indefinitely, so growth has no built-in end point and a lost part can simply be built again from the nearest surviving one. An animal closes its equivalents down early and is stuck with the body it finished. That is why you can take a cutting from a willow and not from a horse, and why losing an arm is an inconvenience here rather than a catastrophe. Later entries add that the vines have a scent and get used as a herb in Galar (Pokémon Sword & Shield, 2019), which is a fair thing to do with tissue the organism regrows on a daily schedule.
### END

### tangrowth
binomial: Tangrowth vine-perennis
plant_analogue: Established perennial vine thickets
genetic_concept: Biomass accumulation over time
note: Bulk without a trunk changes what the wood is for. A tree's xylem has to hold the tree up, so its conduits stay narrow and numerous; a climber has been relieved of that duty and builds the widest vessels in the plant kingdom — approaching half a millimetre across in some lianas, single tubes running metres without a join. Flow scales with something near the fourth power of the radius, so the plumbing is extravagant. It is also brittle in one specific way: a wide vessel embolises easily when sap freezes or comes under drought tension, and one bubble takes out the entire tube. Lianas thin out sharply where winters are cold, stopped by their own pipework.
habitat_note: A specimen defined by sheer accumulated bulk deserves the oldest question asked about it. In the seventeenth century van Helmont potted a willow sapling, weighed it and weighed the soil, watered it for five years and weighed both again: the tree had put on some seventy-five kilograms and the soil had lost a few ounces. He concluded the mass had come from the water, which was most of the way to right. It comes from water and from carbon dioxide out of the air, assembled by light. The overwhelming majority of a large plant was, quite recently, gas — and the soil is not the material, only the workshop.
record: The entries keep returning to disposability: arms lost to predators do not trouble it and grow back at once (Pokémon Diamond & Pearl, 2007), and where growth is fastest it shortens its own vines by tearing parts off (Pokémon Sword & Shield, 2019). That last detail is the interesting one, because plants really do amputate on purpose. Cladoptosis is the deliberate shedding of whole living branches at a pre-formed break point, complete with the sealing tissue that closes the wound — some poplars, cottonwoods and swamp cypress do it routinely. The logic is accounting rather than injury: a limb that has been shaded out by the growth above it stops repaying what it costs to maintain, so the tree cuts it loose and puts the nitrogen somewhere useful. The clean, self-pruned lower trunk of a forest-grown tree is the record of that decision made repeatedly. Losing a limb is not something that happens to a plant so much as something it does.
### END

### exeggcute
binomial: Exeggcute ovulum-sextus
plant_analogue: Multi-seeded fruit clusters
genetic_concept: Seed clustering & clonal grouping
note: Six of them sharing one shell has a straightforward botanical name. Sow a citrus pip and it often comes up as several seedlings at once, because the seed carries more than one embryo: alongside the single embryo made by fertilisation, the maternal tissue lining the seed spawns extras that are clones of the mother tree. They germinate together and compete, and the clones usually win — which is why an orange grown from a pip so often comes true when it has no business doing so. It also means a seed is not necessarily one individual, and the things inside it are not necessarily siblings. Some of them are the parent.
habitat_note: The entry saying that a lost member is replaced by morning points at the strangest capability plants have. Take a fully differentiated cell from a carrot root — a cell whose job was settled long ago and which is doing nothing but storing sugar — put it in the right medium, and it will divide, disorganise into a formless mass, and then reconstitute an entire carrot plant with roots, shoots and flowers. Steward demonstrated it in 1958 and the whole of plant tissue culture follows from it. Animal cells do not do this; a differentiated animal cell has burned its bridges and needs serious intervention to be talked back. A plant cell keeps the whole genome available and, released from the tissue telling it what to be, will simply start over. Losing a piece is a survivable accident for a plant in a way it is not for anything else here.
record: The entries wobble on what it even is — often mistaken for eggs, but discovered to be a life-form more like plant seeds (Pokémon FireRed & LeafGreen, 2004) — and they are consistent that the shells matter: very durable, and able to survive cracking without spilling the contents (Pokémon Gold & Silver, 2000). A seed coat is the most over-engineered structure most plants build, and the reason is that it has two jobs which pull in opposite directions. It has to keep the embryo intact, dry and dormant through whatever the interval demands, and then it has to fail, on cue, at the right moment. Many legumes solve the first so thoroughly that they cannot solve the second: the coat is genuinely waterproof, and the seed cannot germinate at all until fire, frost heave, gut acid or abrasion breaches it. That is why gardeners nick or soak hard seed, and why some species will sit viable in soil for decades waiting for something to damage them enough to let them live.
### END

### exeggutor
binomial: Exeggutor palma-cephala
plant_analogue: Coconut palm (Cocos nucifera)
genetic_concept: Regional phenotypic variation
note: The regional variant has a real counterpart, and the coconut's is written into its genome. The species falls into two populations, Pacific and Indo-Atlantic, separated long enough to be told apart genetically and by the shape of the nut — and the boundary between them follows the routes people sailed rather than the way the currents run. Which is the awkward part of a plant famous for drifting. A coconut does float for months, sealed around its own fresh water for germination on a beach that has none. But the varieties that fruit early on a short trunk, the ones anybody would choose to carry aboard, are the ones found where the boats went.
habitat_note: Several heads that must agree is a better description of a plant than of an animal, and the difference is real rather than poetic. A tree is modular — built from repeated units, each with its own leaves, buds and vascular supply — and the integration between those units is partial. A shaded branch runs a different budget from a lit one on the same trunk, exporting nothing and eventually being written off and shed. Fruit on one side of an apple can be starved while the other side is loaded, because the sugar mostly travels within a sector rather than being pooled. Damage one root and the branches above it, not the whole crown, are the ones that suffer. There is no centre issuing orders, and no single account. A plant is closer to a federation than an individual, and the question of how much its parts cooperate is answered separately, branch by branch.
record: Reported in almost every generation since 1998, and two claims recur throughout all of it. The first is light: heads grow larger under strong sunlight (Pokémon Ruby & Sapphire, 2003), a good environment produces more of them (Pokémon Crystal, 2001), and cloudy days leave it sluggish (Pokémon Sword & Shield, 2019) — which is light-limited photosynthesis stated about as plainly as a game can state it. The second is shedding: a head that grows too large drops off and continues as an Exeggcute (Pokémon Red & Blue, 1998). That is vegetative reproduction rather than metaphor. Kalanchoe drops fully rooted plantlets from its leaf margins, and garlic and Agave form bulbils that fall from the stalk and grow where they land. Producing offspring by dropping a piece of yourself is ordinary plant practice.
### END

### chikorita
binomial: Chikorita foliolum-primus
plant_analogue: Seedling / juvenile leaf stage
genetic_concept: Juvenile vs. adult leaf morphology
note: Juvenile foliage can differ from adult foliage so completely that the two have been catalogued as separate species, and one plant is still doing it for a reason that no longer exists. The New Zealand lancewood spends its first fifteen years as a stiff whip hung with hard, mottled, downward-pointing leaves, then switches abruptly to soft plain ones held upward once it clears about three metres. The best explanation is the moa, a browsing bird that fed up to roughly that height and has been extinct for six hundred years. The tree has not been informed. Leaf shape is a record of what used to eat the plant, kept long after the eating stopped.
habitat_note: The leaf on this specimen's head is the right place to say that a seedling's first leaves usually aren't leaves. Cotyledons form inside the seed, before there is any such thing as a shoot, and in most plants they are storage organs — fat, plain, shaped nothing like the foliage to come, and steadily emptied and discarded as the seedling draws them down. The first true leaf is the first thing a plant builds rather than spends, and it arrives only once the reserves are nearly gone. Every plant in this room began by running on a packed lunch, and the moment that runs out is the moment it has to start earning.
record: The claim repeated across five generations is oddly specific: the leaf checks humidity and temperature (Pokémon Gold & Silver, 2000), and the Pokémon uses it to seek out warm places (Pokémon Crystal, 2001). Leaves do both, and they are better at it than the phrasing suggests. Stomata respond directly to the dryness of the air rather than to the water in the soil, closing within minutes as the vapour pressure deficit rises — the leaf is measuring humidity and acting on the measurement. Temperature is not merely sensed either, it is managed: transpiration is evaporative cooling, and a leaf in full sun can sit several degrees below the surrounding air because of it, or well above it when the stomata shut. There is a long-running argument that leaves hold themselves nearer a photosynthetic optimum than the air around them would allow. Waving a leaf about to read the weather is a fair description of what one is doing anyway.
### END

### bayleef
binomial: Bayleef foliolum-secundus
plant_analogue: Aromatic foliage (bay laurel, eucalyptus)
genetic_concept: Secondary metabolite production
note: The scent is also a message, and not every recipient is a plant. A leaf being chewed gives off a volatile blend specific to what is chewing it — the plant reads elicitor chemistry in the caterpillar's saliva and adjusts the mixture accordingly — and parasitic wasps use that blend to locate the caterpillar. The plant cannot remove the animal itself, so it publishes its position to something that can. Neighbours downwind eavesdrop on the same signal and have their own defences part-way switched on before anything reaches them. A smell coming off damaged foliage is closer to an alarm call than to a leak.
habitat_note: Aromatic leaves are a delivery system, not a perfume. The volatile oils in a bay laurel are not loose in the tissue — they sit in discrete oil cells and glands, sealed away from the plant's own metabolism, which is why an intact leaf on the tree barely smells and a crushed one fills a room. The scent is what a rupture releases, and rupture is what a mouth does. Everything we call flavour in a herb is a compound that was formulated to be objectionable, packaged for release at the exact moment something bites down, and then cultivated by us for precisely that property.
record: The entries insist on the aroma and on its effect: a spicy scent from the leaves around its neck that acts as a stimulant and restores health (Pokémon Gold & Silver, 2000), a fragrance that makes people peppy (Pokémon Ruby & Sapphire, 2003). Worth correcting one thing first — an essential oil is not an oil. It is a mixture of volatile terpenes, which is why it evaporates and why it is captured by steam rather than pressed. And the effect these compounds are actually built for is aimed downward rather than at people. Many are allelopathic: they wash off the foliage, accumulate in the soil beneath the plant, and inhibit the germination and root growth of anything trying to start there. Eucalyptus and several sages produce conspicuously bare ground around themselves this way. A bracing smell in a kitchen is, in the field, a chemical exclusion zone.
### END

### meganium
binomial: Meganium corolla-magna
plant_analogue: Mature flowering canopy trees
genetic_concept: Floral maturity & scent signaling
note: A canopy in flower is usually a canopy that agreed on a date. The dipterocarp forests of Southeast Asia bloom in mass events years apart — hundreds of species across whole regions flowering within weeks of one another, cued by a cool spell or a drought, then nothing again for two to seven years. The logic is arithmetic. Nothing that eats the seed can hold a population together on a food supply that disappears for years at a stretch, so the empty years starve the predators and the flowering year buries the survivors in more seed than they can get through. Blooming in unison is how a slow organism outnumbers a fast one.
habitat_note: Floral scent is an address rather than a broadcast, and the addressing is fine-grained: blends are tuned to what the plant is trying to attract, which is why a moth flower and a fly flower smell so unlike each other, and why one smells of carrion. The detail worth having is what happens afterwards. A flower that has been pollinated frequently stops advertising — emission falls away within hours, colour can change, and the insect traffic redirects to blooms still worth visiting. Scent is a running cost, and a plant switches it off the moment the transaction has closed.
record: The strangest claim in the record is also the most literal-minded: its breath has the power to revive dead grass and plants (Pokémon Gold & Silver, 2000). Read as chemistry rather than magic it is nearly reasonable, because a plant's breath is what other plants eat. Carbon dioxide is the raw material of photosynthesis and it is genuinely in short supply — at around four hundred parts per million, it is the limiting ingredient for many crops on a bright day, which is why commercial glasshouses pipe it in deliberately and run at two or three times outdoor concentration for measurable gains in yield. Exhaling on a plant is a real if microscopic favour. The other repeated claim, that the flower's fragrance calms aggression (Pokémon Gold & Silver, 2000), sits on far shakier ground, and the honest position is that inhaled plant volatiles have measurable effects on people that fall a long way short of the ones folklore assigns them.
### END

### hoppip
binomial: Hoppip pappus-minimus
plant_analogue: Dandelion & thistle achenes
genetic_concept: Wind-mediated seed dispersal (anemochory)
note: The parachute is mostly hole, and that is the trick. A dandelion pappus is around ninety per cent empty space, and the air passing through it does not simply leak away — it separates into a ring-shaped vortex that sits detached in the wake, a little above the bristles, and stays there. The arrangement generates roughly four times the drag of a solid disc of the same width for a fraction of the material, and nothing engineers have built flies this way. It is stable, silent, weighs a few hundred micrograms, and it exists so that something which cannot move can put its offspring somewhere it has never been.
habitat_note: The entries have it gripping the ground to avoid being blown away, which is the right instinct for the stage it is at. A dispersal structure only works if it is released at the correct moment, and a great many plants hold on hard until then. Dandelion florets are gripped at the base of the head until the pappus is fully dry and expanded; poppy capsules do not open at all but perforate near the top and shake seed out only when the stem is bent far enough by wind, which is a genuine threshold switch; and hygroscopic bracts across the composites close in damp air and open in dry. The common feature is that the plant is not merely letting go — it is refusing to let go until the conditions clear a bar. Something small and light that has to cling to the ground on a windy day is at the stage before dispersal, and holding on is the whole job.
record: The entries are consistent that it is at the mercy of the air — carried off on the gentlest breeze, possibly as far as the next town (Pokémon Crystal, 2001) — and add that clustering is the defence (Pokémon Gold & Silver, 2000). What surprises people about small, light, wind-carried things is where they end up. Fine particles caught in a rising thermal can be lifted into the free atmosphere and travel for days: viable fungal spores, pollen, seeds and whole small invertebrates are routinely sampled kilometres up, and the flux across an ocean is not zero. Saharan dust reaches the Amazon and the Caribbean every year in quantities measured in millions of tonnes, and there is biology in it. The last entry has it as a herald of spring when it gathers in fields and mountains (Pokémon Diamond & Pearl, 2007), which is also how airborne pollen behaves — arriving in a mass, on the season, from somewhere upwind.
### END

### skiploom
binomial: Skiploom pappus-medius
plant_analogue: Maturing seed head
genetic_concept: Dispersal-stage transition
note: The stage between flowering and flying is not idle time. A dandelion shuts its head once the florets are done and stays shut for days while the seed finishes and the pappus is assembled inside, then reopens as something else entirely: a dry sphere with no reward on offer to anyone. The stalk does its own work in that interval. Having flowered close to the ground where it was sheltered, it elongates and lifts the head clear of the surrounding grass before opening, sometimes doubling in height. Releasing into still air at ankle level would waste the whole apparatus, so the plant stands up first.
habitat_note: This is the specimen with a stated threshold, and thresholds are how plants make decisions with no nervous system to make them. The flower opens above about eighteen degrees and the extent of the opening tracks the temperature, which is exactly the behaviour of a real thermonastic bloom: crocus and tulip open and close on differential expansion between the two faces of the petal, and a fraction of a degree is enough to start it moving. The value of a threshold is that it converts a continuous variable into a yes or no without any deliberation — the tissue simply responds, and the response is the decision. Most plant behaviour is built this way. What looks like a judgement about whether it is warm enough to be open is a mechanical property of the petal, tuned so that the answer comes out right often enough to be worth having.
record: The entries are unusually precise, and precision invites checking: the flower blossoms above sixty-four degrees Fahrenheit, the degree of opening tracks temperature, and it is sometimes used as a thermometer (Pokémon Ruby & Sapphire, 2003). Plants have been used exactly that way, and the practice is older and more useful than it sounds. Phenology aside, gardeners have read soil temperature off lilac and forsythia for generations, and agricultural extension services still publish planting advice keyed to what is in flower rather than to the calendar, because the plant integrates soil warmth, air temperature and season in a way a thermometer at chest height does not. Sixty-four Fahrenheit is about eighteen Celsius, which is a reasonable figure for a spring flower's opening threshold. The entry is not describing a gimmick; it is describing a plant used as an instrument, which is a real and widespread agricultural habit.
### END

### jumpluff
binomial: Jumpluff pappus-maximus
plant_analogue: Fully dehisced dandelion seed head
genetic_concept: Terminal dispersal morphology
note: Terminal morphology still has one moving part. A dandelion pappus closes when the air is damp — the bristles fold inward as humidity rises, cutting the drag and refusing the flight, then open again as it dries. The mechanism is built into the tissue and needs no signal to run it, and what it amounts to is the seed declining to launch into weather that would bring it down early or rain that would pin it flat. Something falling at thirty centimetres a second in dry air is not committed to flying. It is committed to flying on a good day.
habitat_note: The entries have it crossing oceans on seasonal winds and coming down when it meets cold air, which is a fair account of how a light thing actually travels. Long-distance flight is not a matter of staying up but of being lifted: warm air rising off ground heated by the sun carries small particles upward far faster than they settle, and once above the friction layer they move with the weather rather than the breeze. Cold air is the end of it — a descending, stable air mass has no lift in it, and whatever was riding comes down. That is why long-range biological transport is seasonal and directional rather than random, and why the plants that go furthest are not the ones with the best equipment but the ones releasing seed at the time of year when the atmosphere is doing the work.
record: The entries are the boldest dispersal claim in the collection: it rides warm southern winds across the sea to foreign lands and descends when it meets cold air (Pokémon Ruby & Sapphire, 2003), circling the globe on seasonal winds and scattering spores as it goes (Pokémon Diamond & Pearl, 2007). Scaled down to something with no muscles it is close to true, and the evidence is in where plants have got to. Ferns and mosses, whose spores are dust, have colonised remote islands far more thoroughly than seed plants have, and a great many fern species are shared between continents with no other explanation. The trade is severe. A spore that light carries essentially no reserves and lands with nothing, so most of them die, and the ones that succeed do so because the numbers are absurd — a single fern frond releasing millions in a season. Crossing an ocean is not the difficult part. Arriving with enough to start is.
### END

### sunkern
binomial: Sunkern semen-quiescens
plant_analogue: Dormant sunflower seed
genetic_concept: Seed dormancy
note: Waiting is a capability, and it scales further than seems reasonable. In 1879 the botanist Beal buried twenty bottles of seed in a Michigan sand lot to find out how long they would keep. The experiment is still running, dug up on a schedule now stretched to twenty years, and mullein from the bottle opened in 2021 germinated after a hundred and forty-two years underground. A date palm has been grown from a seed recovered at Masada and dated to around two thousand years old. None of that is sleep. It is a position being held, at a metabolic rate low enough to be mistaken for death.
habitat_note: Dormancy is a decision rather than a pause. A dormant seed is metabolically awake enough to be reading conditions and declining to act on them, and it holds out for a specific cue: a long cold for many temperate species, scarring of the coat for others, or the karrikin compounds in smoke that tell a fire-country seed the canopy above it has just burned away. Held right, the wait can be extraordinary — a date palm has been grown from seed roughly two thousand years old, and Silene from tissue buried in Siberian permafrost for some thirty thousand. What looks like a dead thing on a shelf is a plant holding its position.
record: Two claims worth separating. The first is dietary and, remarkably, defensible: it lives by drinking only dewdrops from beneath leaves and is said to eat nothing else (Pokémon Gold & Silver, 2000). Dew and fog are genuine water sources rather than a rounding error. Namib desert plants live on fog, and coastal redwoods take up water directly through their foliage when the fog rolls in — foliar uptake, running the transpiration stream backwards, and in a dry summer it can supply a substantial fraction of what the tree uses. Leaves drink. The second claim is stranger: that it drops out of the sky, and that a year after a cold summer their population explodes (Pokémon Diamond & Pearl, 2007). Seed rain is real, and so is the observation underneath it — seed production responds to the previous season's weather, so a cohort arriving now is a report on a summer already gone.
### END

### sunflora
binomial: Sunflora helianthus-erectus
plant_analogue: Sunflower (Helianthus annuus)
genetic_concept: Heliotropism & phototropic growth
note: What reads as one flower is upward of a thousand of them. A sunflower head is a packed disc of individual florets, each with its own seed to make, opening in rings from the rim inward over several days — a colony working to a schedule rather than a bloom. The packing is the famous part. Each new floret is placed about a hundred and thirty-seven degrees around from the one before it, and that angle, being the worst possible approximation to any simple fraction, is the only one at which the spirals never line up and leave a gap. The head is full precisely because no two florets could agree on a direction.
habitat_note: The sunflower's tracking is a clock, not a reflex. A young head swings east to west through the day and then rotates back overnight to be facing east again by dawn, and it will keep running that cycle for a while even under constant light, because it is driven by the plant's own circadian rhythm rather than by simply leaning at whatever is bright. Then the tracking stops. A mature head locks facing east permanently, which warms it fast in the morning — and a warm east-facing head receives several times the pollinator visits of one turned west. The plant stops following the sun at exactly the point that standing still pays better.
record: The record is consistent that it runs on light and stops without it: sunlight converted into energy, petals closed and the body still after dark (Pokémon Gold & Silver, 2000), and later, always looking toward the sun (Pokémon Black 2 & White 2, 2012). Worth putting a number on how good that conversion is, because the answer is humbling. The theoretical ceiling for turning sunlight into plant matter is around eleven per cent, and almost nothing gets close: a productive crop over a full season converts something like one to two per cent of the light falling on it into biomass, and most vegetation manages less. A commercial solar panel on the same patch of ground will beat it several times over. Photosynthesis is not an efficient process. It is a spectacularly successful one, which is a different property, and the difference is that it builds its own collectors out of the product.
### END

### bellsprout
binomial: Bellsprout caulis-carnivorus
plant_analogue: Pitcher plant seedling stage
genetic_concept: Root-to-shoot resource allocation
note: Carnivory pays under a narrow set of conditions, and the arithmetic is what explains the spindly root. A trap is a leaf that has surrendered most of its photosynthesis, so the nitrogen it catches has to buy back more carbon than the modification cost — which happens only where the soil is poor enough for nitrogen to be genuinely limiting and the light and water are abundant enough that carbon is not. Shade such a plant, or fertilise the ground under it, and the trade stops paying almost at once. Roots are what an organism builds when the soil is worth reaching into.
habitat_note: The trap on a pitcher plant is not an organ the plant invented from nothing — it is a leaf, and you can watch it become one. A Nepenthes leaf develops as a broad flat blade that narrows into a tendril at the tip, and the pitcher forms at the very end of that tendril as a swelling that hollows out, so the whole structure is a single leaf doing three jobs along its length: photosynthesis at the base, climbing in the middle, and digestion at the end. Seedlings begin with small simple pitchers sitting almost on the ground and produce progressively more elaborate ones as they mature, often switching shape entirely between the lower and upper parts of the same plant. Carnivory here is not an addition to the plant's design. It is the far end of a leaf, modified until it can eat.
record: The entries insist on the roots as much as the trap: a carnivorous Pokémon that traps and eats bugs, using its root feet to soak up moisture (Pokémon Red & Blue, 1998), planting its feet deep underground to replenish water and unable to escape while rooted (Pokémon Gold & Silver, 2000). The vulnerability is the honest part. Being anchored is what makes everything else in this collection necessary — a plant cannot leave, cannot hide, and cannot chase, so every problem it has must be solved in place and in advance. Chemical defence exists because flight does not. Seed dispersal exists because the parent cannot move to better ground. Almost every strategy catalogued in this building is a workaround for the same constraint. A predator that cannot run from anything is an unusual predator, and it is the ordinary condition of a plant.
### END

### weepinbell
binomial: Weepinbell caulis-praedator
plant_analogue: Pitcher plant (Nepenthes) trap
genetic_concept: Carnivory as nutrient-poor-soil adaptation
note: Several pitchers have stopped catching anything. Nepenthes lowii grows a stiff splayed vessel with a lid that secretes a substance tree shrews come to lick, positioned so the animal has to sit over the mouth to reach it — the plant is collecting droppings, and takes more nitrogen that way than its insect-eating relatives manage. A related species grows a slender pitcher that a bat roosts inside, with a structure at the back shaped to throw the bat's echolocation back at it, and is paid in guano. Having built a vessel for digesting animals, the surest way to fill it turned out to be feeding them.
habitat_note: A pitcher does not grab, snap or close. It is a slide, and the engineering is in the rim. The peristome — the flared collar around the mouth — is covered in microscopic ridges and channels that hold a film of water by capillary action whenever it rains or when the plant's own nectar glands are secreting, and a wetted peristome becomes genuinely frictionless to an insect's adhesive foot pads, which work by surface tension and cannot grip a surface that is already wetted. Ants walking the rim in dry conditions are perfectly safe; the same ants on the same rim after rain aquaplane straight in. Some species even switch the trap off and on, staying dry for hours so that scouting ants return to recruit the colony, then wetting when the crowd arrives. Nothing about it is a mechanism in the moving-parts sense. It is a surface that stops working underfoot.
record: The entries have it dissolving prey with strong acids while remaining unharmed itself, because it also oozes a neutralising fluid (Pokémon Gold & Silver, 2000). Self-protection from your own digestion is a genuine problem and pitcher plants solve it by where things happen rather than by neutralising anything. The fluid sits in a sealed vessel that is dead tissue at the point of contact, and the enzymes are secreted into that space rather than made where they could do harm — the same principle as the compartmented poisons in this room's wall text, one step further along. The fluid itself is stranger than acid. In several Nepenthes it is viscoelastic, thickened by long-chain polymers so that it behaves like slime rather than water: an insect that falls in cannot swim, because every movement drags the whole fluid with it. Drowning in something that will not let go is a different mechanism from being burned, and it is the one that does most of the work.
### END

### victreebel
binomial: Victreebel caulis-voraciosus
plant_analogue: Mature pitcher plant with lure scent
genetic_concept: Chemical mimicry for prey attraction
note: Luring usually means advertising a reward with no intention of paying it. One species is unusually literal instead. Nepenthes albomarginata carries a narrow band of white edible hairs just below the rim, and termites of one particular species come to harvest it in columns; the foraging party works its way around the band, the footing gives out, and a single pitcher can take hundreds of them in a night. It is the only plant known to bait a named prey species with a food crop grown for the purpose. The rest of the genus runs a confidence trick. This one keeps its promise and eats the customers.
habitat_note: Pitcher fluid is not a sterile vat, and the things living in it are the surprise. A great many pitchers host a permanent community — mosquito and midge larvae, mites, rotifers, sometimes a small crab spider fishing from the rim — that swim in the digestive fluid unharmed and shred the drowned insects into pieces the plant can absorb faster than its own enzymes would manage. The relationship is real enough to measure: pitchers with their infauna removed digest more slowly and take up less nitrogen. Some species have effectively outsourced the entire process, secreting almost no enzymes of their own and relying on bacteria and larvae to do the work. The trap that swallows everything turns out to be running a small ecosystem inside itself, and to be worse at its job without it.
record: The entries build a nastier picture than the others: prey lured with an aroma of honey and swallowed whole, melted in a day, bones and all (Pokémon Yellow, 1999) — and then the detail that acid which has dissolved many prey becomes sweeter and therefore more effective at attracting the next (Pokémon Gold & Silver, 2000). That last one has a real counterpart, and it works the other way about. Pitcher plants do not sweeten with use; they do, however, benefit from having caught things before, because a working pitcher accumulates the bacterial and invertebrate community that processes its catch, and an established trap is more effective than a fresh one. There is also decent evidence that the volatiles rising off a pitcher's contents attract further insects — the smell of previous victims is part of the lure. The entry has the mechanism backwards and the observation right: an old trap does work better.
### END

### treecko
binomial: Treecko lacerta-viridis
plant_analogue: Chlorophyll-pigmented epidermis
genetic_concept: Camouflage via convergent pigmentation
note: Almost nothing that looks like a plant is green the way a plant is green. Vertebrate greens are generally a trick of layers — a blue produced by structure in the skin laid over a yellow pigment — because animals do not make chlorophyll and mostly cannot make a green pigment at all. The exceptions are worth the detour: the pea aphid carries carotenoid-making genes it appears to have taken from a fungus outright, and a sea slug eats algae, keeps the chloroplasts alive inside its own tissue and runs on them for weeks. Matching a leaf is easy. Matching it honestly means stealing the machinery.
habitat_note: Worth pausing on why a plant-coloured animal is green in the first place. Chlorophyll absorbs strongly in the red and the blue and reflects the middle of the spectrum straight back, so a leaf is green precisely because green is the part of the sunlight it declines to use — and awkwardly, green is close to where the sun's output peaks. The best current explanation is not about maximising capture at all but about stability: a pigment system tuned to the peak would be violently sensitive to every flicker of passing cloud and shade, and giving up the middle buys a steadier supply. The colour is a compromise, which makes it the right colour for this room.
record: The entries are precise about the feet: small hooks on the soles that let it scale vertical walls (Pokémon Ruby & Sapphire, 2003), later described as countless tiny spikes enabling it to walk on ceilings (Pokémon Diamond & Pearl, 2007). Climbing plants have solved the same problem twice over and neither answer is a hook. Ivy sends out short adventitious rootlets that flatten against the surface and secrete a glue loaded with nanoparticles, which sets hard and grips brick, bark and glass — the residue is what stays behind when ivy is pulled off a wall. Virginia creeper does something more deliberate still: its tendril tips swell into adhesive pads that only develop once they have touched something, so the plant commits the glue after contact rather than before. A gecko sticks by physics and a vine sticks by chemistry, and the vine gets to stay there permanently.
### END

### grovyle
binomial: Treecko lacerta-silvestris
plant_analogue: Canopy-adapted foliage
genetic_concept: Habitat-matched morphology
note: The canopy is less the top of a forest than another floor of it, and it has its own soil. Epiphytes — mosses, ferns, bromeliads — accumulate along the big branches, catch litter and dust, and build mats of humus tens of centimetres deep, with their own invertebrates and their own water supply. Trees in wet montane forest then grow roots up into it: adventitious roots emerging from the branch itself, turning into the mat, and mining the crown for nutrients the tree dropped there in the first place. It is a tree feeding on its own compost, several storeys above the ground it is rooted in.
habitat_note: Look up through a stand of tall trees and the crowns often do not touch: each is held back from its neighbours by a clean gap, so the canopy reads as a jigsaw with the sky showing along every join. Crown shyness, and after decades it is still not fully settled. Abrasion explains some of it — branches in wind sand each other's growing tips off — but shy crowns turn up in sheltered stands too, and there is decent evidence that trees detect a neighbour by the far-red light reflecting off its leaves and simply stop growing toward it. A canopy is more negotiated than it looks from underneath.
record: The entries put it in the canopy and keep it there: leaves that camouflage it from enemies in the forest (Pokémon Ruby & Sapphire, 2003), a life spent flying between branches in dense jungle (Pokémon Diamond & Pearl, 2007). Camouflage is the wrong word for what a forest actually does to colour, though, and the real effect is more useful. Light that has passed through leaves is not merely dimmer, it is altered — chlorophyll strips out the red and leaves the far-red largely untouched, so shade is measurably redder than open sky in a way plants can read. They read it constantly. The ratio of red to far-red is how a plant detects that it is under a neighbour, and a low ratio triggers shade avoidance: stems elongate, branching is suppressed, flowering is brought forward. A seedling knows it is being overshadowed before it is even short of light, because the colour of the shade has already told it.
### END

### sceptile
binomial: Treecko lacerta-arborea
plant_analogue: Woody seed pods
genetic_concept: Structural lignification
note: Lignin is the compound that let plants stand up, and it is also close to indigestible — a tangled phenolic mesh laid through the cell wall with no repeating link for an enzyme to take hold of. There is a well-loved story that this is where coal came from: trees evolved wood before anything evolved a way to rot it, so the Carboniferous forests simply piled up unconsumed. It is a good story and it is probably wrong. Fungal lignin-degrading enzymes appear to be older than the coal, which is better explained by wet subsiding basins in the right place at the right time. Wood was never inedible. It was buried.
habitat_note: A big tropical tree stands up in a way that has nothing to do with going deep. Rainforest soils are thin and the nutrients sit near the surface, so the roots stay shallow and the tree solves stability above ground instead: buttresses, thin vertical flanges spreading out from the base like guy ropes turned to wood, sometimes several metres tall and only a few centimetres thick. They work in tension on the windward side rather than by weight, which is why they are blades rather than columns. The largest trees in the wettest forests are held up by geometry, not by anchorage.
record: The reports stay remarkably consistent across two decades: leaves sharp enough to cut through thick trees (Pokémon Ruby & Sapphire, 2003), seeds carried on its back said to be full of nutrients that revitalise trees (Pokémon Ruby & Sapphire, 2003), and a note that it regulates its body temperature by basking in sunlight (Pokémon Emerald, 2005). The sharp leaves are the least fanciful part — real grasses load their tissue with silica, laying down microscopic phytoliths that stiffen the blade and abrade the mouthparts of whatever grazes on it, which is why grass can genuinely cut skin. The basking is ordinary too: leaves manage their own temperature by angling toward or away from the sun and by transpirational cooling, so a plant positioning itself in sunlight to warm up is describing standard leaf behaviour.
### END

### seedot
binomial: Seedot glans-pendulus
plant_analogue: Acorn (Quercus)
genetic_concept: Mast fruiting
note: An acorn is far too heavy to fly, and oaks moved north after the ice at hundreds of metres a year, which took some explaining. The answer has feathers. A jay caches acorns one at a time, pushed into the soil at about the depth a seedling wants, several thousand in an autumn, carried as much as a few kilometres from the parent tree — and it remembers where most of them are, but not all. The forgotten fraction is the oak's dispersal system, and it is a selective one: jays reject infested acorns and take the largest they can carry. The tree is not being robbed. It is paying a courier in the goods being shipped.
habitat_note: An acorn cannot be dried, and that single fact shapes what an oak can do. Most seeds are orthodox — they can be dehydrated to a few per cent moisture, frozen, and kept for decades in a vault. Acorns are recalcitrant: they stay alive only while they stay damp, they are killed by drying and killed again by freezing, and they must germinate within months of falling. So there is no seed bank for oaks, no long wait in the soil, no insurance copy in a freezer in Svalbard. An oak's entire strategy is to make enormous numbers of short-lived seeds and get them into the ground immediately.
record: The entries describe two quite different things and only one of them is an acorn. It looks exactly like an acorn dangling from a branch and startles anything nearby by moving (Pokémon Ruby & Sapphire, 2003) — but the same entry says it attaches itself to the branch by the top of its head and sucks moisture out of the tree, growing glossier the more it drinks. That is not an acorn's relationship with an oak; it is a parasite's. Real plants do it and one does it with unsettling competence. Dodder germinates as a thin thread with almost no chlorophyll and a few days of reserves, and it locates a host by smell — it grows toward the volatiles of a suitable neighbour, discriminating between species — then coils on and drives haustoria through the bark into the vascular tissue, tapping water and sugar directly. Its own root then dies. A seed-shaped thing hanging from a branch and drinking is a fair picture of it.
### END

### nuzleaf
binomial: Seedot glans-erectus
plant_analogue: Sprouting acorn / sapling
genetic_concept: Germination & apical growth
note: One bud at the top runs the whole plant, and it runs it by suppression. Auxin moving down from the tip holds the buds below it dormant — not by starving them but by withholding the vascular connections they would need in order to grow — which is why a plant left alone makes a single leader, and why pinching that leader out makes it bush within days. Take the top off a spruce and the nearest whorl of branches competes for the vacancy; one turns upward and becomes the new trunk, leaving a permanent kink at the point where the argument was settled. A tree's shape is a record of which tips were in charge, and when.
habitat_note: A seedling's first act is performed in the dark, and it produces a different body. Germinate a seed under soil and it does not make a small green plant — it makes an etiolated one: a long pale stem racing upward, leaves undeveloped and folded away, the tip bent into a protective hook that drags rather than pushes through the grit, and no chlorophyll made at all, because chlorophyll in darkness is cost without return. That form is not a failure to develop. It is a distinct developmental programme, run deliberately until the hook breaks the surface and a photoreceptor registers light, at which point the whole thing switches over within hours: the hook opens, the stem slows, the leaves expand and the green comes on. Every plant in this collection began by growing one body underground and then abandoning it.
record: The entries return again and again to a sound: a flute made from the leaf on its head, whose song leaves listeners uneasy (Pokémon Ruby & Sapphire, 2003), and a habit of living deep in forests, in holes bored into large trees (Pokémon FireRed & LeafGreen, 2004). A blade of grass really is an instrument, and a fairly exact one — stretched taut between the thumbs it works as a double reed, the two edges of the blade beating against each other in the airstream, which is the principle of an oboe rendered in something disposable. The forest matters too, and not in the direction most people assume. Dense vegetation is a poor reflector and a good absorber of high frequencies, so sound travelling any distance through woodland arrives stripped of its top end, noticeably muffled and low. A thin reedy note carrying further than it should through trees is the kind of thing a forest genuinely does to sound.
### END

### shiftry
binomial: Seedot glans-arborifex
plant_analogue: Wind-dispersing mature tree
genetic_concept: Fan-shaped leaf adaptation for wind capture
note: A leaf's response to wind is to stop being a fan. Broad leaves reconfigure as the air speeds up — curling back, rolling into a cone, gathering into streamlined bundles — so drag climbs far more slowly with wind speed than flat-plate arithmetic says it should, which is the only reason a tree in a gale keeps its foliage at all. The longer-term response is structural. A sapling shaken daily grows shorter and thicker than one left alone, and trees raised in still air have to be staked, having never built for a load they were never given. Catching wind is easy. Surviving having caught it is the design problem.
habitat_note: Most plants shut their stomata at nightfall, and a surprising number do not. Measure water loss in darkness across a broad range of species and it keeps turning up — sometimes a few per cent of the daytime rate, sometimes a fifth of it — spent under conditions where no carbon whatever is being fixed in return. That looks like straightforward waste and it remains genuinely unresolved. The leading explanations are that a transpiration stream running overnight keeps nutrients moving up from the roots, and that a plant with its pores already part-open can begin photosynthesising the instant light arrives rather than losing the first of the morning to opening up. Both may be true. What the measurements settle is that the night shift in this room is not simply a shutdown: a fair number of plants leave the doors open in the dark and pay for it.
record: The entries are specific enough to check: fans generating gusts close to a hundred feet per second, and a home atop towering trees dating back over a thousand years (Pokémon Ruby & Sapphire, 2003), later credited with levelling houses (Pokémon Diamond & Pearl, 2007). A hundred feet per second is about thirty metres a second, roughly seventy miles an hour — a severe gale, and close to where shallow-rooted conifers begin to be thrown wholesale. The leaves are the more interesting half, because a fan is near the largest a leaf can usefully be. A big blade holds a thick layer of still air against its surface, and still air is an excellent insulator, so a large leaf in full sun cannot shed heat and cooks. That is why enormous leaves belong to the humid tropics and the shaded understorey, and why desert foliage is reduced to slivers. It runs the other way after dark: the same poor coupling to the air lets a broad leaf radiate to a clear night sky and fall several degrees below the surrounding temperature, which is how frost forms on foliage on a night the thermometer never quite reaches freezing.

### lotad
binomial: Lotad nymphaea-minor
plant_analogue: Water lily pad (Nymphaea)
genetic_concept: Aquatic leaf buoyancy
note: A floating leaf is also a snorkel, and in some water lilies it is a pump. The young leaves of a yellow water lily draw air in and hold it slightly above atmospheric pressure — driven by the temperature and humidity difference across the leaf rather than by anything muscular — and that pressure pushes a steady flow down the petiole, through the buried rhizome, and out again through the older leaves, which vent. The plant ventilates its own roots in mud with no oxygen in it, using warmth as the pump and last season's leaves as the exhaust. Buoyancy is only the visible half of the arrangement.
habitat_note: A floating leaf is an engineering problem with a very specific solution set. It has to stay on the surface, shed water off the top so the stomata are not flooded, survive being trodden on by whatever lands on it, and resist tearing when the pond gets rough. A water lily pad answers all four: the stomata migrate to the upper surface, which is the reverse of every land plant; the top is waxy and self-cleaning so rain beads off; the underside carries reinforcing ribs radiating from the stalk; and the whole thing sits on trapped gas. Victoria amazonica takes it to the point of absurdity with a two-metre disc, an upturned rim, and a girder-like lattice of ribs beneath that lets it carry a distributed load of tens of kilograms. Paxton, who first flowered it in England, said outright that the Crystal Palace roof came from studying its underside.
record: Two entries, and read together they are a small essay on the cost of a leaf. It grows weak if its broad leaf dies, and travels overland in search of clean water (Pokémon Ruby & Sapphire, 2003); and it is said to have lived on land before, returning to the water because the leaf on its head grew too large and heavy to carry (Pokémon HeartGold & SoulSilver, 2010). That second claim is the right way round. A leaf on land has to be held up, and holding it up is the single largest structural expense a plant has — which is precisely why an aquatic plant can afford a disc a land plant of the same size could never support, because the water carries it instead. The trade is real and it is one-way: haul that leaf out and there is nothing underneath it. A plant that grew a leaf too heavy for land and went back to the water has not degenerated. It has stopped paying for scaffolding.

### lombre
binomial: Lotad nymphaea-media
plant_analogue: Emergent aquatic vegetation
genetic_concept: Aquatic-to-terrestrial transition
note: Plants living at the waterline build two kinds of leaf and choose between them as the level moves. Submerged, an amphibious species makes thin, finely divided foliage with no cuticle worth the name, taking its gas straight out of the water; the same stem above the surface makes something else entirely — flat, waxed, stomata on the underside, and stiff enough to hold itself up now that the water has stopped doing it. The switch is chemical, driven largely by abscisic acid, and it runs in whichever direction the pond does. A flood does not kill this kind of plant. It changes what the plant builds next.
habitat_note: The slippery film the entries keep mentioning is doing real work in a river. Submerged plants and algae secrete mucilage, and in flowing water it is not incidental — it reduces drag, keeps the surface from being colonised by everything looking for something to grow on, and holds a thin layer of water against the tissue so that a plant briefly exposed at low flow does not dry out. It also gives silt somewhere to settle, which is why river weed is always slightly muddy to the touch. The wider point is that life in moving water is dominated by shear rather than by depth. River plants are strap-shaped, flexible and streamlined, and they survive by bending flat into the current rather than resisting it — a rigid plant in a flood is a lever with a root at the end of it, and it goes.
record: The entries put it firmly at the margin and firmly on a schedule: it lives at the water's edge where it is sunny, sleeps by day on a bed of water grass and becomes active at night (Pokémon FireRed & LeafGreen, 2004), feeding on the aquatic mosses growing on rocks in the riverbed (Pokémon Emerald, 2005). Mosses on submerged stone are a real and under-noticed community, and they are engineers rather than passengers. A cushion of aquatic bryophyte on a boulder slows the water immediately around it, traps fine sediment and organic particles out of the flow, and creates a still, packed, oxygenated space that supports an invertebrate density far higher than the bare rock beside it. Strip the moss and the animals go with it. In fast upland streams where there is little else for anything to eat or hide in, the moss on the rocks is most of the habitat.

### ludicolo
binomial: Lotad nymphaea-maxima
plant_analogue: Fully emergent wetland plant
genetic_concept: Environmentally triggered phenotype switch
note: Some plants decide what sex to be each year, on the evidence of how the last one went. Jack-in-the-pulpit comes up male while its underground corm is small and female once it is large, and it moves between the two over a lifetime: a good season builds reserves, so it flowers female, sets fruit at considerable expense, and may return male the following spring having spent itself back below the line. habitat_note: An emergent plant is the third of the three postures in this room, and the most successful. Rooted in the anoxic mud, stem and leaves entirely in the air, it gets the best of both — a water supply that never fails and unrestricted access to carbon dioxide and light — provided it can solve the plumbing, which it does with air channels running the whole way down. Reeds do it so well that a reedbed is among the most productive vegetation on the planet, and the roots pump enough oxygen into the sediment around them to sustain bacteria that break down what is dissolved in the water. That is not an analogy for a treatment works; it is one. Constructed reedbeds are used as functioning sewage and runoff treatment across Europe, and what does the work is oxygen delivered to mud through a plant that had to solve its own suffocation first.

What is worth adding here is why the myth is so durable. The original experiments were uncontrolled — the same person watered, tended and observed the plants they were playing music to — and plants respond dramatically to handling, watering regime and air movement, all of which vary with how much time someone spends near them. The effect was real and the cause was the gardener. It is a good reminder for a collection like this one that an observation can be entirely honest and still be about the observer.

### roselia   [DRAFT — please review]
binomial: Roselia rosa-gemina
plant_analogue: Garden rose (Rosa)
genetic_concept: Flower color genetics (anthocyanin pathway)
note: Carrying both a red and a blue flower on one stem is a fair stand-in for anthocyanin pigment genetics in roses: the same biosynthetic pathway, tuned by pH and modifying genes, can shift a bloom's color across the red-to-blue spectrum within one cultivar line.
habitat_note: Its entries mention flowers turning up in unusual colours, which is the right prompt for the most famous failure in plant breeding. There is no blue rose and there never has been, because roses lack the enzyme that makes delphinidin, the pigment responsible for blue in delphiniums, violas and cornflowers. Centuries of breeding could not produce what the pathway cannot build, and the lavender-grey things sold as blue are the closest conventional selection can get. The eventual answer was transgenic: a pansy gene inserted to supply the missing enzyme, released commercially in 2004. It is still not blue. Pigment colour depends heavily on the pH of the vacuole it sits in and on the metal ions present, and a rose petal's cell sap is too acidic — the same delphinidin that reads blue in a cornflower reads mauve in a rose. Colour is chemistry and plumbing together.
record: The entries pair two things that seem unrelated and are not: the aroma brings serenity and grows stronger as the plant gets healthier (Pokémon FireRed & LeafGreen, 2004), while the thorns contain a vicious poison (Pokémon Ruby & Sapphire, 2003). Scent as a health indicator is defensible — volatile production is metabolically expensive and drops off sharply in a stressed or malnourished plant, so a strongly scented specimen genuinely is a well-fed one. The prickles are the part worth correcting. A rose does not have thorns. A thorn is a modified shoot with vascular tissue running into it, as on hawthorn; a spine is a modified leaf, as on a cactus; and what a rose carries is a prickle, an outgrowth of the epidermis alone with no vascular connection to anything — which is exactly why it snaps off cleanly under a thumbnail while a hawthorn thorn does not. Three different organs, one word in common usage, and the rose has the flimsiest of them.

### budew   [DRAFT — please review]
binomial: Roselia rosa-clausa
plant_analogue: Unopened rosebud
genetic_concept: Pre-bloom bud dormancy
note: A closed bud that releases pollen-like powder when disturbed captures a rosebud's pre-anthesis state, when reproductive tissue is fully formed but held closed until temperature and moisture cues call for the bud to open.
habitat_note: Its entries have the bud closing over winter and opening to the sun's warmth, and the bud is doing more work than it appears to. What overwinters is not a dormant lump but a fully formed miniature: in most temperate woody plants next year's shoot, leaves and often flowers are laid down inside the bud during the previous summer, then sealed under overlapping scales, waxed, and in many species insulated with dense hair. The pollen that reaches noses in bulk comes from the plants nobody notices flowering: grasses, and wind-pollinated trees like birch, hazel and plane, whose flowers are small, green, unscented and utterly unmemorable. Roses get blamed every year and are close to innocent. The general rule is reliable — if a flower is worth looking at, its pollen is not in the air, because the plant has arranged for something else to carry it.

### roserade   [DRAFT — please review]
binomial: Roselia rosa-duplex
plant_analogue: Bicolor rose cultivar
genetic_concept: Polymorphic floral traits
note: Two different flower-hands, one sweet-scented and one sharply toxic, mirrors how closely related rose cultivars can diverge sharply in scent-compound and toxin production despite near-identical floral structure — a case of polymorphism at the biochemical level.
habitat_note: A specimen with two different hands is the right one for a real and elegant piece of floral engineering. Primroses come in two forms, and once you know it you cannot stop seeing it: pin flowers show the stigma at the mouth of the tube with the anthers hidden halfway down, and thrum flowers do the exact reverse. Pollen from a pin lands on the part of an insect that will touch a thrum's stigma, and vice versa, so the arrangement mechanically routes pollen between the two morphs and away from the flower it came from. Darwin worked it out and regarded it as one of his more satisfying results. It is backed up chemically too — pollen landing on the wrong morph frequently fails to germinate. Two hands carrying different equipment, each useless on its own and effective on the other, is very close to literally what a heterostylous species is doing.
record: The entries are precise about the asymmetry: each hand contains different toxins (Pokémon Platinum, 2009), and the more toxic it is the sweeter its aroma (Pokémon HeartGold & SoulSilver, 2010). Carrying more than one poison at once is standard practice and it is not redundancy. A mixture is harder to evolve resistance to than any single compound, because a herbivore must solve several unrelated chemical problems simultaneously rather than one; mixtures also frequently act synergistically, with one component blocking the detoxification pathway the animal would use on another. Plants routinely deploy dozens of related compounds from the same family for exactly this reason, varying the blend between tissues and over time. The honest note on the second claim: sweetness and toxicity do sometimes travel together, since both are costly and both track the plant's condition, but there is no rule saying a stronger scent means a stronger poison, and relying on that outdoors would be a mistake.

### cacturne   [DRAFT — please review]
binomial: Cacnea opuntia-nocturna
plant_analogue: Nocturnal desert cactus (Saguaro-type CAM plants)
genetic_concept: CAM photosynthesis
note: Becoming active only at night mirrors Crassulacean Acid Metabolism (CAM), used by most cacti: stomata stay shut in the punishing daytime heat and open only at night to fix CO2 with minimal water loss, then run photosynthesis internally come daylight.
habitat_note: The entry that has it sit motionless through the day so as not to lose moisture is describing the deepest setting this strategy has. Under ordinary drought a CAM plant shifts its gas exchange into the night. Under severe drought it stops exchanging altogether: the stomata stay shut around the clock while the plant recaptures the carbon dioxide from its own respiration and cycles it internally, acidifying overnight and de-acidifying by day with nothing entering or leaving. CAM idling gains it precisely nothing. What it does is keep the machinery ticking over and protected from a light it cannot use, so that when rain finally arrives the plant is ready in hours rather than having to rebuild — and it will hold that state for months, in some species for a year or more. A cactus doing nothing visible in the heat is not dormant. It is sealed, and running.
record: The record is consistent for two decades that this is a night animal: unmoving through the day to avoid losing moisture and active once the temperature drops (Pokémon Ruby & Sapphire, 2003), hunting prey exhausted by the day's heat (Pokémon FireRed & LeafGreen, 2004). What actually fills a cactus is stranger than sand and considerably more useful. The flesh is loaded with mucilage, a polysaccharide gel that holds water far more tenaciously than free liquid would, resists evaporation from a cut surface, and gives the tissue its characteristic slime — which is why a severed pad seals over and survives rather than draining. It binds well enough that prickly pear mucilage has been used as a flocculant to clear muddy drinking water, dragging suspended particles out of it. The blood is not sand. It is glue, and it is why the plant can hold thirty days of water without simply leaking it.

### turtwig   [DRAFT — please review]
binomial: Turtwig testudo-foliata
plant_analogue: Shell as living topsoil
genetic_concept: Rhizosphere / soil microbiome
note: A shell literally made of packed soil that supports a growing leaf models the rhizosphere — the thin zone of soil around roots that plants actively condition with exuded sugars to cultivate a beneficial microbial community.
habitat_note: The soil on this specimen's back is the right prompt for what soil actually is, which is mostly not solid. A good topsoil runs around half mineral grains and organic matter by volume, and the other half is pore space — split, roughly evenly and constantly shifting, between water and air. That air is not incidental. Roots respire, and they need oxygen down there to do it, which is why drowning a pot plant kills it: waterlogged ground has not been made too wet so much as it has had the air driven out of it, and the roots suffocate in a medium that appears to be nothing but abundance.

True, and the scale of it historically is hard to overstate — atmospheric oxygen is entirely biological in origin, the by-product of a reaction that was after the hydrogen, and its accumulation two and a half billion years ago poisoned most of what was alive at the time. But the modern version of that claim needs a correction that comes up every time a forest is called the lungs of the planet. A mature forest consumes very nearly as much oxygen as it produces, because everything living in and under it — roots, animals, and above all the fungi and bacteria decomposing the litter — respires the same oxygen back out again. Forests are close to neutral. What actually put the oxygen there was carbon that got buried instead of being decomposed, and most of it was marine.

### grotle   [DRAFT — please review]
binomial: Turtwig testudo-silvestris
plant_analogue: Established shrub root mass
genetic_concept: Root system establishment
note: Branches and a bigger canopy sprouting from its shell reflect how, once a root system is established enough to secure water reliably, a plant shifts investment from root growth to aboveground canopy expansion.
habitat_note: Established is doing a lot of work as a description, because the absorbing surface of a root system is almost entirely temporary. The uptake happens through root hairs — single epidermal cells drawn out into tubes, each living a few days before collapsing and being replaced further along the growing tip. The classic count, made on one four-month-old rye plant, came to something over fourteen billion of them, with a combined length in the hundreds of kilometres, all packed into about a twentieth of a cubic metre of soil. A root system is not a fixed structure that gets bigger. It is a wave, continuously rebuilt at its edge.

A root tip can detect a moisture gradient and bend toward the wetter side, and specific genes have been identified in that response, but gravity generally wins the argument, and the sensing works over short distances rather than across a landscape. The popular version is worse still. Roots are famous for finding and breaking drains, and the usual reading has that backwards: the pipe was leaking first, the moisture and nutrients drew roots to the joint, and the growth then widened a fault that already existed. A root does not divine water. It grows everywhere it can afford to and thickens wherever it finds any.

### cherubi   [DRAFT — please review]
binomial: Cherubi prunus-gemina
plant_analogue: Cherry fruit cluster
genetic_concept: Fruit ripening signaling (ethylene)
note: Two cherries, one described as still gathering nutrients, models asynchronous fruit ripening within a cluster — driven in real Prunus species by localized ethylene signaling that can trigger one fruit to ripen ahead of its neighbor.
habitat_note: Ripening runs on a gas, which is the strange part. Ethylene is the only plant hormone that is a gas at ordinary temperatures, so a fruit that starts to ripen does not merely ripen — it emits, and the emission ripens everything nearby, which is exactly why one bad apple takes the box and why an unripe avocado sitting in a paper bag with a banana comes good. The discovery came from street lighting: nineteenth-century towns noticed that trees near leaking gas lamps dropped their leaves out of season, and the culprit turned out to be a trace of ethylene in the coal gas, doing to trees what fruit does to fruit.

Leaves are sources, exporting sugar; developing fruit, roots and new shoots are sinks, drawing it in, and the pull is competitive — a heavy fruit set will starve the shoot growth beside it, and removing fruit visibly redirects the plant into wood. The classic demonstration is brutal and simple: cut a ring of bark from a branch, severing the phloem, and everything above the cut carries on feeding the fruit while everything below is cut off from it. Growers still girdle deliberately to force sugar into a crop. The ball emptying itself into the plant is exactly the right picture of where a fruit's contents come from.

### servine   [DRAFT — please review]
binomial: Snivy serpens-scutifera
plant_analogue: Waxy cuticle leaves
genetic_concept: Cuticle wax biosynthesis
note: Leaf-like growths described as blade-sharp point to real cuticle biology: many mature leaves synthesize a thick, stiff wax cuticle layer via CER-family genes that both reduces water loss and adds rigidity — sharp edges are a plausible mechanical side effect.
habitat_note: A waxy cuticle is the reason there is anything on land to catalogue. The first plants out of the water faced one overwhelming problem — evaporation — and the answer was to coat themselves in a waterproof layer of cutin and wax. It worked, and it created the next problem immediately: a surface that will not let water out will not let carbon dioxide in, so a sealed plant is a starving one. Stomata are the resolution, adjustable pores punched through the seal, and the two inventions only make sense as a pair. Every plant in this room is a waterproof object with thousands of controllable leaks in it.
record: One entry, and it is a good one: when it gets dirty its leaves cannot be used in photosynthesis, so it keeps itself scrupulously clean (Pokémon Black 2 & White 2, 2012). The problem is real — dust on a leaf shades it and blocks the stomata — and the solution plants arrived at is the reason the lotus is a symbol of purity. A lotus leaf is covered in microscopic waxy bumps, themselves furred with finer structures, so a water droplet cannot wet the surface and sits on the tips as a near-sphere. It rolls off at the slightest tilt, and because it touches so little of the leaf and so much of any dirt on it, it picks the dirt up and carries it away. The leaf is not washed; it is swept by its own repellence. The effect was measured with electron microscopy in the 1970s and has been sold as self-cleaning paint and glass ever since. Fastidiousness about leaves is engineering rather than vanity.

### serperior   [DRAFT — please review]
binomial: Snivy serpens-regalis
plant_analogue: Ancient/long-lived tree
genetic_concept: Longevity & growth-rate trade-offs
note: Said to move without expending energy, a stylized nod to how the most long-lived plants (bristlecone pines, some yews) grow extremely slowly, trading fast biomass gain for cellular efficiency and multi-century lifespan.
habitat_note: The trade-off worth naming here is written into wood. A tree that grows fast makes light, low-density timber — wide vessels, thin walls, cheap volume — and wins the race to the light by simply being taller sooner; balsa is the extreme. A tree that grows slowly makes dense wood that resists rot, insects and breakage, and lives for centuries while the fast ones fall over. Neither is the better answer, because they are answers to different questions: gaps in a canopy reward speed, closed forest rewards persistence. Long-lived and fast-growing is not a combination anything has managed, and the density of a piece of wood tells you which bargain the tree struck.
record: The entries credit it with taking in solar energy and boosting it internally (Pokémon Black & White, 2011). Plants would like to, and largely cannot, which is the more interesting fact. Photosynthesis saturates: past a fairly modest light level the machinery is running flat out and extra photons add nothing, so a leaf in full midday sun is receiving several times more energy than it can use. That surplus is dangerous rather than wasted — energy with nowhere to go generates reactive oxygen and damages the apparatus that absorbed it — so plants spend real effort throwing it away. Non-photochemical quenching converts the excess to heat within seconds, pigments shift to bleed it off, and chloroplasts physically slide to the sides of the cell to hide from the light. A leaf in bright sun spends much of the day defending itself from its own supply, and the limiting resource is almost never the sunlight.

### grookey   [DRAFT — please review]

### ferroseed   [DRAFT — please review]
binomial: Ferroseed spina-metallica
plant_analogue: Burr & bur-seed (Xanthium, Arctium)
genetic_concept: Epizoochory (hitchhiking seed dispersal)

### breloom   [DRAFT — please review]
binomial: Shroomish fungus-pugnax

habitat_note: Its dex credits it with enriching the forest, and the mechanism is less genteel than fallen leaves. A caterpillar eats a leaf and returns most of it within a day or two as frass, and frass is a far better fertiliser than litter is: it arrives already broken down, rich in nitrogen and in a form soil microbes can use straight away, and it arrives in midsummer rather than waiting for autumn. The uncontroversial half is that herbivores accelerate cycling — chewed leaves and frass return nitrogen to the soil in weeks rather than holding it in the canopy until autumn, and in outbreak years the pulse is large enough to show up in stream chemistry downhill. The contested half is whether the plants come out ahead. Grazing optimisation, the proposal that moderate herbivory lifts productivity above what the plant would have managed unbrowsed, has support in some grassland systems and has been argued over for decades, because partial compensation is easy to demonstrate and genuine overcompensation is not. ### whimsicott   [DRAFT — please review]
binomial: Cottonee gossypium-volans
plant_analogue: Wind-dispersed cotton/willow seed fluff
genetic_concept: Anemochory (wind dispersal)
note: Said to ride the wind and vanish without a trace, this maps onto anemochory in fluffy-seeded plants like cotton and willow, whose lightweight seeds are built almost entirely around catching a breeze.
habitat_note: A specimen with no fixed address is the right place for the rarest and most consequential kind of dispersal. Almost every seed lands close to its parent, but the interesting arrivals are the ones that should not have happened at all — and they are how remote islands acquired their floras. The Hawaiian archipelago sits several thousand kilometres from anywhere, and its enormous native flora is generally reckoned to descend from a few hundred original colonisations across tens of millions of years: roughly one successful arrival per hundred thousand years, mostly carried in or on birds, the rest by drifting on the sea or riding a storm. Nothing about that is a reliable mechanism. It is a very unlikely event given enough time, and the whole botany of an ocean is built from it.
record: The entries make a point of its rootlessness — it does not live in a fixed location, floating around on whirling winds and turning up all over the place (Pokémon Ultra Sun & Ultra Moon, 2017), occasionally carried right across the world by a hurricane. Dispersal distances behave in a way that makes this less contradictory than it sounds. Measure where seeds land and the distribution is severely lopsided: the overwhelming majority fall within a few metres of the parent, and then there is a long thin tail of individuals that go orders of magnitude further. The average is close to useless as a summary, because everything ecologically interesting happens in the tail — colonising new ground, crossing a barrier, shifting a range as climate moves. A species is characterised less by how far its seeds usually go than by how far the rare ones occasionally do, which is why a plant can look sedentary for a century and still be found on the next island.

### petilil   [DRAFT — please review]

### foongus   [DRAFT — please review]
binomial: Foongus fungus-mimicus
plant_analogue: Deceptive floral mimicry
genetic_concept: Batesian-style mimicry
note: Disguising itself as a Poké Ball to lure trainers is a playful version of deceptive floral mimicry, where some orchids mimic a female insect or a rival flower's reward to trick pollinators into visiting for nothing in return.
habitat_note: Pretending to be something dangerous only works while the pretence is rare, and that constraint is the whole reason this kind of mimicry stays in check. Batesian mimicry — a harmless thing wearing the warning colours of a harmful one — depends on predators having learned that the signal means trouble, and they learn from experience. If mimics become common relative to the models, most encounters with the signal turn out harmless, the association weakens, and the protection degrades for the mimic and the model alike. The system is therefore frequency-dependent and self-limiting: the mimic can only ever be a minority. It is one of the few places in ecology where a strategy's success is capped by its own popularity, and it explains why a convincing imitation of something dangerous is usually not very common.

What real organisms mimic is what has been in their environment for a long time: other species, substrate, dung, dead leaves, and the eyes of larger animals. The one genuine category of human-driven mimicry is agricultural, where weeds converge on the crop they hide among because we weeded out the ones that stood out. ### chesnaught   [DRAFT — please review]
binomial: Chespin castanea-bellator
plant_analogue: Mature, fully lignified seed defense
genetic_concept: Maximal structural defense investment
note: Encased in what's essentially a full suit of chestnut-bur armor, this final stage is the endpoint of investing heavily in structural defense — energy spent lignifying a tough husk is energy not spent on additional seed production.
habitat_note: This specimen is the pioneer bargain refused, and refusing it has a price. Carbon spent on armour is carbon not spent on height, and in a race decided by who is above whom that is a straightforward loss — which is why the pattern across species is so consistent. Fast colonisers are poorly defended and mostly get away with it, because they are dead within a few seasons anyway and being eaten matters less than being late. Under load, wood does not snap cleanly like a ceramic — the fibres buckle, split along the grain and pull apart cell by cell, and every one of those small failures absorbs energy. That is why a wooden shield outperforms a stone one, why timber-framed buildings ride out earthquakes that drop masonry, and why the specific energy absorbed by balsa in a crushing test is high enough that it is still used as a core material in blast and impact panels. They are also close to pure cellulose, around ninety per cent and upward, which is unusual: most plant fibre comes bundled with lignin and has to be rotted or chemically stripped before it is any use, whereas a cotton hair arrives essentially ready. That purity is why this became the world's fibre rather than one of the many stronger ones. It needed the least done to it.
record: The entries hold the two uses side by side: seeds carried on the cotton fluff and spread on the wind so that other plants and Pokémon benefit, and the same cotton spun into a glossy yarn that is a regional specialty (Pokémon Sword & Shield, 2019). The second use has a history stranger than it looks. Cotton was domesticated four separate times, on three continents, by people with no contact with one another — twice in the Old World from diploid species in Africa-Arabia and South Asia, and twice in the New World from tetraploids in Mesoamerica and coastal South America. Four independent human populations found the same genus, noticed the same overgrown seed hairs, and arrived at spinning them. The New World species carry a genome formed when an African cotton somehow crossed the Atlantic and hybridised with an American one, long before any person was involved. The plant made the journey first.

### calyrex   [DRAFT — please review]
binomial: Calyrex rex-vegetalis

The last millimetre of a growing root carries a cap of cells that is doing several jobs at once: it detects gravity, using dense starch grains that fall through the cell and settle against whichever wall is now downward, so the plant reads its own orientation from where the ballast lands. The same tip senses touch and swerves around stones, tracks moisture gradients, registers the chemistry of what it is growing into, and secretes a slime that lubricates its passage and feeds the bacteria it wants around it. ### meowscarada   [DRAFT — please review]

### dolliv   [DRAFT — please review]

### capsakid   [DRAFT — please review]

### scovillain   [DRAFT — please review]

And then spruce, which cannot start on bare gravel but grows straight through the alder it has been waiting for, shades it out and inherits the ground it was given. The pioneer's contribution is the exact thing that ends its tenure.
record: The entries treat it as a luxury good, and the last one puts a price on it: essential oils made from its flowers have a sublime scent and are staggeringly expensive (Pokémon Sword & Shield, 2019). Real floral oils are, for reasons of sheer arithmetic. Rose otto takes something on the order of three to four tonnes of petals to yield a single kilogram of oil, and the petals have to be picked by hand in the few hours after dawn, before the day's heat drives off the volatiles the whole business exists to capture. Jasmine is worse, because heat destroys the scent outright and the flowers have to be extracted with solvent rather than distilled. Orris, the violet note in perfumery, comes from iris rhizomes that are dried and then left to age for three to five years before they smell of anything at all. Nothing about it is difficult chemistry. It is that the raw material is enormous, fragile, and on a schedule.

### decidueye-hisui   [DRAFT — please review]
binomial: Rowlet strix-decidua subsp. frigida
plant_analogue: Cold-climate ecotypes
genetic_concept: Behavioural divergence without morphological change
note: Almost nothing measurable separates this form from the standard one: four hundred grams, identical height, a few points shuffled between stats. What changed is behaviour — and that gap is a real and frequently missed distinction. Populations of a single species in different climates often diverge in habit, timing and tolerance while remaining almost indistinguishable on a specimen sheet, which is precisely why herbarium material alone can hide an ecotype. The body records some differences and simply does not record others.
habitat_note: The claim that a cold-country form differs from its lowland relatives is testable, and the test that settled how to do it is a century old. Clausen, Keck and Hiesey took single plants of yarrow and cinquefoil, split each one into cuttings — genetically identical clones — and planted the pieces at three sites along a transect from the California coast to the high Sierra. Genuine genetic ecotypes and mere plasticity separate cleanly under that design: a lowland clone raised at altitude stays lowland-shaped and usually dies, while a montane clone grown at sea level keeps its montane habit. What came out was that populations of a single widespread species are quietly differentiated all along the gradient, each locally adapted and none of it visible without the transplant. A regional form is not simply a plant in a colder place. It is a population that has been selected in one, and a common garden is how you can tell.
record: The entries keep making the same claim, which is speed and precision — a quill nocked and fired in a tenth of a second (Pokémon Ultra Sun & Ultra Moon, 2017), shots that pierce a pebble at over a hundred yards (Pokémon Sun & Moon, 2016). Plants shoot as well, and they are quicker than this. The sandbox tree builds pressure in a drying capsule until it detonates, throwing seed at something like seventy metres a second and clearing forty metres of ground; bunchberry opens its flower and fires pollen in under half a millisecond, a couple of hundred times faster than the tenth of a second an archer is being praised for. The Hisuian entry goes further, offering the air stored in its feather shafts as firm proof that evolution can be influenced by environment (Pokémon Legends: Arceus, 2022). That deserves a small correction: environment does not direct the change, it selects among changes already present. Plants do bend the point, though — one genotype will grow quite different leaves above and below the waterline.

### zarude-dada   [DRAFT — please review]
binomial: Zarude simius-liana var. nutrix
plant_analogue: Nurse plants and provisioned seed
genetic_concept: Parental investment as a life stage
note: The rarest entry in the collection, because on every measurable axis nothing differs at all — same mass, same height, same stats, same typing. The distinction is that this one is raising a child. Parental care is a life stage rather than a form, and plants keep their own version of it: a seed is provisioned with endosperm it did not earn, and a nurse plant shelters seedlings beneath its canopy at real cost to its own growth. Investment in offspring does not show up on a measuring tape.
habitat_note: Provisioning is how a plant equips its offspring for the dark, and the currency is seed size. A species can make a great many small seeds or a few large ones, and it cannot do both — the trade-off is one of the most consistent in plant ecology. Small seeds travel further and arrive in numbers, and they need a gap in the canopy and light within days or they are finished. A large seed is a packed lunch: the seedling emerges with weeks or months of reserves and can survive in deep shade on the forest floor, growing slowly under a closed canopy until an opening appears overhead. That is why the biggest seeds in the world belong to shade-tolerant forest trees and the smallest to orchids and weeds of open ground. Parental investment in a forest is measured in how long the young can afford to wait in the dark.
record: One entry does the work, and it is generous: once the vines on its body tear off they become nutrients in the soil, and this helps the plants of the forest grow (Pokémon Sword & Shield, 2019). Woody climbers are not usually so good for a forest, and the measurements are fairly unforgiving. A tree carrying a heavy load of lianas grows measurably more slowly, reproduces less, and dies sooner — the climbers compete above ground for light and below ground for water, and they add weight and windage to a crown that was not built for it. Worse, they link neighbouring crowns together, so a falling tree drags others down with it. Liana abundance has been rising across neotropical forests for decades, and because climbers store far less carbon than the trees they suppress, a forest shifting toward lianas holds less carbon than the one it replaced. Vines shed into the soil do return nutrients. It is the standing part of the plant that costs the forest.

### shaymin-sky   [DRAFT — please review]
binomial: Shaymin gratia-florens var. volatilis
plant_analogue: Wind-dispersed seed (pappus, samara)
genetic_concept: The dispersal–defence trade-off
note: Twenty-five points off each defence, twenty-seven onto speed, and flight gained — armour exchanged for air. Dispersal morphology makes the same bargain in real plants: a seed built to travel is a seed built light, and the pappus, plume or wing that buys distance is mass not spent on a thick protective coat. Heavy armoured seeds drop beneath the parent and wait; light ones leave and take their chances. Things that move far are rarely well defended.
habitat_note: Its land form is filed with the pollinators; this one is the same organism after it has committed to the air, which makes it the room's clearest statement of the trade. Flight equipment is dead weight in every sense — a pappus, a wing or a plume is tissue built at the plant's expense that contributes nothing to the seedling once it lands, and every milligram of it is a milligram not stored as reserves. So the two forms of the same species are the two ends of one decision. Heavy, well-provisioned seed that stays close and can start under a canopy; or light, far-travelling seed that arrives somewhere new with nothing in the bank and needs open ground and immediate light to survive at all. The gracidea does not change what the organism is. It changes which side of that bargain it has taken, and this collection files the two answers in different rooms.
record: The claim is the boldest in the room: it can dissolve toxins in the air and instantly transform ruined land into a field of flowers (Pokémon Diamond & Pearl, 2007). Plants do clean air, and the honest version needs care in both directions. Indoors, the famous chamber experiments showing houseplants stripping volatile organic compounds have been badly over-read — repeat the calculation for a real room with real air exchange and you need something on the order of a hundred plants per occupant to rival simply opening a window. Outdoors is a different matter. Roadside vegetation and green walls measurably lower particulate concentrations downwind, tree canopies intercept dust, and the effect scales with leaf area and hairiness rather than with charm. One plant on a desk is decoration. A belt of them along a road is infrastructure.

### ogerpon-wellspring-mask   [DRAFT — please review]
binomial: Ogerpon larva-persona var. aquifera
plant_analogue: Mycorrhizal root partnerships
genetic_concept: Tolerance conferred by a symbiont
note: Height, mass and every stat are identical to the unmasked form — one external component is exchanged and the organism's relationship with water changes completely. Real plants acquire tolerances the same way, by partnership rather than by their own genome. Mycorrhizal fungi thread out from the root into soil the root itself could never reach, and a colonised plant can draw water from pores far too fine for its own tissue, holding on through drought that kills its uncolonised neighbours. The plant did not change; its partner did.
habitat_note: Its note has the symbiont; the wetland case for one is worth stating, because tolerance here is frequently rented rather than owned. Most mycorrhizal fungi need oxygen and desert their hosts in waterlogged ground, so wetland plants tend to run with far lower colonisation than their upland relatives — and where the partnership does persist, it is usually because the plant is supplying the air. Oxygen pumped down through aerenchyma into the root zone keeps a thin oxidised sheath around the root, and the fungus lives in that, on gas the plant delivered. It is an unusually explicit arrangement: the host is not merely feeding its symbiont carbon, it is ventilating the room. Change the water table and the whole thing reorganises, which is why drainage alters a wetland's fungal community as sharply as it alters the plants standing in it.
record: The single relevant entry is also the room's thesis: its type changes based on which mask it is wearing (Pokémon Scarlet & Violet, 2022). Nothing in botany changes an organism's identity that cleanly, but one thing comes disconcertingly close. Graft a cutting onto a rootstock and the two normally stay distinct, one above the join and one below. Very occasionally the cells of the two intermingle at the union and grow on together as a single shoot — a graft chimera, one plant with two genomes woven through its tissues rather than blended. The famous one is Laburnocytisus 'Adamii', made in a Paris nursery in 1825 from a laburnum and a purple broom, which grows as one tree and flowers in three ways at once: yellow laburnum on some branches, purple broom on others, and a muddy coppery hybrid-looking bloom in between. It is not a hybrid. It is two plants sharing a body, and which one you get depends entirely on which layer of tissue happened to form the bud.

### ogerpon-hearthflame-mask   [DRAFT — please review]
binomial: Ogerpon larva-persona var. thermophila
plant_analogue: Geothermal-soil panic grass
genetic_concept: Three-way symbiosis and heat tolerance
note: Same body, same mass, a different mask — and the clearest real parallel is one of the strangest results in plant biology. A panic grass growing in Yellowstone's geothermal soil survives root temperatures near 50°C, but only in partnership with a particular fungus, and only when that fungus is itself carrying a particular virus. Strip out either partner and the plant cooks. The heat tolerance belongs to none of the three alone; it exists only in the assembly, which is exactly what a mask that changes what its wearer can survive is describing.
habitat_note: The resist strategy, which is mostly a question of how thick the bark is. Heat kills a tree by cooking the thin ring of living tissue just beneath the surface, and bark is a poor conductor, so the whole problem reduces to insulation and time — a fire that passes in minutes cannot get through several centimetres of it. Giant sequoia carries bark up to about half a metre thick, fibrous and notably low in resin so that it chars rather than burning; longleaf pine wraps its growing tip in a dense tuft of needles for its first years and simply sits at ground level, sometimes for a decade, until it has bark enough to bolt upward past the flame zone. Cork oak goes further and regenerates its bark after it has been stripped, which is why cork can be harvested from a living tree every nine years or so for two centuries.
